How this blend behaves

Estimated from fatty acid profiles

Two different meanings of the same words. The bar labelled Cleansing measures one narrow thing: how much of your fat is lauric and myristic acid. It is not a verdict on the finished soap. Superfat, water and cure all change how the soap actually behaves and none of them touch these five bars, because none of them change which fats are in the pot. Read the bars as a description of the recipe, and superfat as the dose.

Reading these. Each number is simply the percentage of your blend made of certain fatty acids — the ones named in monospace under each bar. They are not scores out of 100 and not marks out of ten. Creamy lather at 37 means 37% of your fats are palmitic, stearic and ricinoleic; it doesn't mean the bar got a B. The shaded band is where most soapers aim, and sitting outside it isn't a mistake — a high-cleansing bar is exactly right for shop hands and wrong for a face. Profiles are typical values; real oils vary by crop, region and refining.

Saponification audit

Every value this calculator uses

Each fat needs a specific weight of lye to saponify, and published values disagree by a few percent because real crops and rendering batches vary. Assuming a value higher than your actual oil overdoses the lye and can leave free alkali in the bar. Assuming one lower only leaves extra fat. So every figure below sits at or near the low end of its published range, checked against SoapCalc, The Soap Kitchen, and the international Codex standards for edible oils and fats (the US Pharmacopeia for castor). Real oils can still come in a little lower, which is why this page never offers less than 5% superfat for bars or 2% for liquid soap, and why the Superfat note under Lye solution on the Recipe tab shows where your batch lands at both ends of the range.

Fat Lye per gram Low – high Spread

Cross-checked against the chemistry. Every value here was also derived independently from each oil's fatty acid make-up, using molecular weights rather than any published chart. Thirteen of the seventeen agree within 3%, which is about as close as this kind of estimate gets. The four that don't, all for known reasons: coconut, palm kernel and babassu carry short-chain acids (caproic, caprylic, capric) that the standard profile leaves out, so the derived figure runs low; shea and rice bran hold several percent unsaponifiable matter that never becomes soap, so their real lye demand sits below the pure-triglyceride estimate; and beeswax is not a triglyceride at all but a wax ester, which is why it takes barely a third of the lye a fat of similar weight would.

For liquid soap, every figure in the table is sodium hydroxide. The page multiplies each one by 1.403 — potassium hydroxide's molecular weight over sodium hydroxide's, 56.106 against 39.997 — then divides by the KOH purity you set, so 90% flakes need about 1.559 times the NaOH figure. The same low-end values carry straight over.

Two to watch. Palm kernel has the widest official range, 0.164 to 0.181. One popular chart lists 0.156, which is below that range and is an error — the same chart’s KOH figure converts to 0.178. The 0.176 used here is the common published value, but it sits about 7% above the official low end, the biggest gap of any fat, so a bar heavy in palm kernel loses the most cushion if a lot comes in low: keep the superfat at 6% or more. Coconut’s published figures disagree more than any other common fat’s; it is used here at the bottom of its official range.

Method

Cold process — pour, then cure

    Before your first batch

    What to have on the bench

    Equipment

    Digital scale reading 0.1 g, 2 kg capacity. Stick blender — not a whisk, you'll be there an hour. Two heatproof containers, stainless or HDPE plastic marked #2 or #5. A silicone spatula. An infrared or candy thermometer. Safety goggles that seal, not glasses. Nitrile gloves. Your mold, lined if it's wood.

    Never aluminum

    Lye reacts with aluminum, releases hydrogen gas and ruins both the pot and the soap. That includes foil, aluminum spoons and some cheap stock pots. Stainless steel, heavy plastic, silicone and glass only — and not thin glass, which thermal-shocks.

    Set up before you mix

    Weigh everything out and line the mold before the lye goes anywhere near water. Once the batch traces you have minutes, not hours, and hunting for a spatula with thickening batter in the pot is how mistakes happen.

    Start small

    Your first batch should be a plain blend with no fragrance and no additives, so you learn what trace looks like without anything accelerating on you. Fragrance is the single most common cause of a first batch going sideways.

    Notes from the bench

    Why the lye number moves

    Every fat needs a different amount of alkali to saponify. Coconut takes 0.178 g of lye per gram; beeswax takes 0.069. Swap 10% of a blend from coconut to shea and the lye requirement drops by half a percent — small, but enough to matter at the margins of superfat.

    Is your mold really 42 oz?

    Check it rather than trust the listing. Line it, fill with water to half an inch below the rim, and weigh the water. Soap batter runs within a few percent of water's density, so that number is your capacity. A mold rated by volume instead of weight will read differently.

    Wet weight vs cured weight

    These weights are wet batter. Bars lose roughly 10–15% of their weight to evaporation over the cure. A 4.6 oz slice comes off the rack near 4 oz — so if you're labeling a net weight for sale, weigh cured bars, not fresh ones.

    Lye purity

    Calculated against 100% pure NaOH. Certified food-grade lye runs 99%+, and anything less means slightly less alkali than the label says — which lands on the safe side again. Don't substitute drain cleaner; the additives aren't disclosed.

    Liquid soap is different: potassium hydroxide is sold at about 90%, the rest being water it holds on to, and this page weighs more of it to make up for that. So set the purity to what your label says. Telling the page 90% when the flakes are really purer overdoses the lye.

    Tallow in liquid soap

    It works, and it's a good use for tallow you already have, but palmitic and stearic acid make cloudy potassium soap that can settle into a white layer. Keep tallow to about a quarter of the fats, let the bottles sit for a week or two and pour the clear soap off the top. Beeswax and the butters don't belong in liquid soap at all.

    Rendering your own tallow

    Leaf fat or suet, rendered wet and strained twice through cloth. Any meat or protein left behind goes rancid in the cured bar and shows up as orange spots six months later.

    Coconut and dry skin

    30% coconut cleans hard — right for shop hands and dishes, drying for some people in the shower. Drop it to 20–25% and give the difference to tallow for a gentler bar.

    Butters and castor

    Shea, cocoa and mango butter belong at 5–15%; past that they mute the lather. Castor stays under 8% or the bar turns sticky and soft.

    When it goes wrong

    It happens to everyone

    Oily liquid on top, or it never sets

    Usually a mis-weighed oil or a blender that never reached real trace. Wait 48 hours before judging — tallow bars often firm up late. If it's still soup, the batch can be rebatched: grate it, melt it down with a splash of water in a crock pot, and remold. Nothing is wasted.

    Crumbly, cracked, or has white pockets

    Too much lye, too little water, or the batch overheated. A crack down the middle means it got too hot — that bar is still usable. White crystalline pockets are unsaponified lye: wear gloves, and don't use that batch on skin. Rebatching fixes it.

    How you know it's safe

    Properly measured soap is safe once cured. If a bar makes your hands feel slippery-slick and tingly after rinsing, or stings, something went wrong — don't use it. Inexpensive pH strips on a wet bar should read about 9 or 10; much above that, rebatch.

    A word on the zap test

    Old-timers touch a cured bar to the tongue to check for lye. It works and it's traditional, but as a beginner don't — a genuinely lye-heavy bar will burn you. Use strips, or simply wait the full cure on a batch you weighed carefully.

    Liquid soap: cloudy, oily or runny

    Milky in the clarity test — not finished, keep cooking. Oily layer after diluting — skim it; next time cook longer or superfat less. Too thick or jelly-like — stir in more hot water, a little at a time. Too thin — stir in a salt solution (1 part salt to 4 parts hot water) a teaspoon at a time; go slowly, because too much turns it cloudy and thin again. Cloudy — usually tallow or other hard fat, and harmless; let it settle and pour off the top.

    Safety

    • Lye burns on contact and fresh solution fumes will catch your throat. Door open or outside, face out of the pot.
    • Goggles, not glasses. Splash goggles that seal around the eyes, plus gloves and long sleeves, every time lye is out.
    • Lye in the eye is an emergency. Flush with plain running water for at least 30 minutes, holding the eyelids open, and take out contact lenses if they come out easily. Then go to the emergency room or an eye doctor right away, even if it feels better — lye keeps damaging the eye after the pain eases.
    • Skin contact: flush with cool running water for fifteen minutes. Not vinegar — neutralizing on skin generates heat on top of the burn. Vinegar is for counters and tools.
    • If lye or raw soap is swallowed: don't make the person vomit, and don't give vinegar or anything else to neutralize it. If they're awake and can swallow, a small glass of water or milk — then call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222, or 911.
    • No kids, no pets, no bare feet in the room while lye is open.
    • Raw soap stays caustic for about 48 hours. Gloves to unmold and cut.
    • Store lye dry and labeled. It pulls water from the air and sets into a brick in a loose container.
    • Keep KOH and NaOH in clearly different containers. Both look like white flakes, and grabbing the wrong one ruins the batch and throws the lye amount off by 40%.
    • Don't breathe pumice dust. Pumice powder can carry crystalline silica, which damages lungs. Wear a dust mask when you measure or pour it, and wipe spills up damp rather than sweeping.
    • Potassium hydroxide gets hotter than sodium hydroxide when it hits water and can boil. Add it slowly, in a container rated for heat, with room to spare.

    Soap-making terms, explained

    Trace, superfat, gel phase and the rest

    Saponification
    The reaction that makes soap. Lye plus fat becomes soap plus glycerin. Once it's finished there is no lye left in the bar — that's the whole point of the cure.
    SAP value
    How much lye one gram of a particular fat needs. Coconut needs 0.178 g, beeswax 0.069. It's why the lye weight changes when you change the blend.
    Superfat
    Deliberately adding slightly less lye than the fats could consume, so a little oil survives unsaponified. 6% means about 6% of your fat stays as free oil. It's insurance against measuring error as much as a feel choice.
    Lye concentration
    How strong you mix the lye water. 31% means the solution is 31% lye and 69% water. Less water means a thicker batter that sets sooner; more water gives you longer to work but a longer cure.
    Trace
    The moment the batter thickens enough that a drizzle off the blender leaves a mark on the surface before sinking. Light trace is the consistency of thin pancake batter and is where you want to pour. If you can stand a spoon in it, you waited too long.
    False trace
    Batter that looks thick but is only cooling hard fats setting up, not saponifying. Common with tallow and beeswax if you soap too cool. Warm it slightly and keep stirring.
    Seize
    Batter that goes from liquid to solid in seconds, usually from a fragrance oil. You glop it into the mold and accept a rustic top. The soap is still fine.
    Gel phase
    The batch heating itself to translucency in the mold, then setting. Gelled soap is slightly darker and harder sooner. Insulating encourages it; it's optional, not required.
    Cure
    Four to six weeks on a rack while water evaporates. Saponification finishes in the first day or two; the rest is the bar getting harder, milder and longer-lasting. Not optional.
    Unmold
    Taking the loaf out of the mold, usually at 24–36 hours, before it's too hard to cut cleanly.
    Potassium hydroxide (KOH)
    The lye for liquid soap. Sodium hydroxide makes hard soap; potassium hydroxide makes soft soap that dissolves in water. They are not interchangeable — KOH molecules are heavier, so a recipe needs about 40% more of it by weight, and it is usually sold at about 90% purity, which this page allows for.
    Paste
    What a liquid soap batch cooks down to before any water is added — a thick, translucent soap like petroleum jelly or taffy. It keeps for months in a closed jar, so you can dilute a little at a time.
    Dilution
    Melting the paste into hot distilled water to make pump soap. Written as paste to water by weight: 1 : 1.5 means 10 oz of paste into 15 oz of water. You can always add more water; you can't take it back out.
    Soap vs. detergent
    Store dish liquid is detergent, made from petroleum or plant chemicals rather than fat and lye. Detergent foams hard and ignores hard water. True soap, like the dish soap this page makes, cuts grease just as well but makes fewer suds and leaves a faint film in hard water, which a little vinegar in the rinse removes.
    Clarity test
    A dab of hot paste stirred into a little boiling water. Clear or faintly hazy means the cook is finished; milky white means keep cooking.
    DOS
    Dreaded orange spots — rancid oil showing through a cured bar months later. Caused by too much polyunsaturated oil, too high a superfat, or tap water.
    Coconut oil, 76° and 92°
    The number is the melt point. 76 degree is the standard soaping grade — solid in the jar, liquid on a hot day — and it's what a supplier ships if you don't specify. 92 degree is hydrogenated to stay solid longer, sold for hot climates; it saponifies identically, it just needs more heat and starts setting again below 92°F, which invites false trace. Unrefined or virgin uses the same numbers and carries a faint coconut smell that mostly fades. Fractionated or MCT coconut oil is not soap oil — the lauric acid has been stripped out, which is the only reason coconut is in your recipe, and its SAP value is different, so it would throw your lye off. If it pours like water at room temperature, it's the wrong one.
    Lauric, oleic, stearic and the rest
    The fatty acids that make up every fat. Lauric makes bubbles and strips oil, oleic makes bars feel soft, stearic and palmitic make them hard and creamy, ricinoleic (castor, almost uniquely) does several good things at once. The five bars on this page are just sums of these.

    Ingredient label

    Heaviest first, the way the rule asks

    Soap

    0 / 1200

    Ordered by weight, heaviest first. Not by the order you added them and not oils-then-everything-else — by what each one actually weighs in the finished bar, which is what the rule asks for. Water is ranked on what is left after the cure, not what went into the jug, and the glycerin the reaction frees is counted and ranked with the rest.

    Dish soap has two extra rules. Strong degreasing soap is the kind the CPSC expects to carry an eye warning, so the dish soap label adds “Avoid contact with eyes. Keep out of reach of children.” And California’s Cleaning Product Right to Know Act covers dish soap (not hand soap) sold there, including online orders shipped there: list the ingredients on the label and on a website, and put a toll-free phone number and web address on the label.

    Call it soap and nothing else. Plain soap sold as soap is exempt from FDA cosmetic rules. The moment the label claims it moisturizes, soothes, heals or treats anything, it becomes a cosmetic or a drug and the rules change. List ingredients in descending order by weight, give a net weight from cured bars, and put your name and address on it.

    Why the odd names. Once lye meets fat there is no tallow or coconut oil left in the bar — there's sodium tallowate and sodium cocoate. Labels naming the raw oils are technically describing something that isn't in the product any more, though plenty of small makers list both.

    Describe it for sale

    Listing, market card or social post — soap words only

    How it stays soap. The writer builds sentences only from what the bar is, how it was made, how it lathers and how it cleans — never what it does to skin. Your own words go through the same check as the bar name, and so do the hashtags. The same rule covers your website, your signs and any customer review you share: the FDA judges the bar by everything you say about it.

    IFRA Category 9

    What the certificate from your supplier means

    The International Fragrance Association sets a maximum usage level for every fragrance material, and those maximums differ by what the product is and how it touches skin. Bar soap sits in Category 9 — rinse-off products for body and hand, alongside body wash, shampoo, bath bombs and salt scrubs. When your supplier sends an IFRA certificate, the Category 9 column is the only one that governs a bar of soap — and liquid hand soap sits in the same category, measured against the diluted soap in the bottle. Dish soap is the exception: it is a household product, so it reads Category 10A — household care products with mostly hand contact — and that column can be lower or higher than Category 9 for the same fragrance.

    How to read the certificate

    It lists maximum percentages by category. Find the Category 9 figure and compare it to what you're using. If it says 8% and you're at 5%, you're fine. If it says 3%, you come down to 3% or you choose a different fragrance. The number is specific to that fragrance and that batch — it is not a general rule you can carry to the next bottle.

    Why some are so low

    Limits drop when a fragrance contains known allergens or sensitisers. Cinnamon, clove, citrus and some florals commonly land between 0.5% and 3%. A low Category 9 number isn't a quality problem with the oil — it's the reason the certificate exists.

    Which amendment

    Standards are revised periodically; the 51st Amendment was published in June 2023, with existing products expected to comply by 30 October 2025. A certificate names the amendment it was written against, so an old sheet for a fragrance you've had on the shelf for years may predate the current limits.

    Is it law?

    Not in the United States. IFRA compliance is voluntary here, but it is the industry's standard of care: wholesale buyers ask for it, product liability insurers expect it, and it is the document you would want to hold if a customer ever reacted to a bar.

    Go to the source. IFRA keeps a searchable Standards Library that stays current — it carries every published standard with a filter for the amendment, so the link does not go stale when the next one lands. Their Understanding the Standards page explains the categories, including what puts bar soap in Category 9. Neither replaces the certificate for your specific fragrance — that comes from whoever sold it to you.

    The measurement trap, and it catches people. IFRA writes its limits as a percentage of the finished product. Soapers habitually think in percentage of oil weight — per pound of oils — and the two are not the same number. A cured bar has lost most of its water, so it weighs appreciably less than the batter you poured, and the fragrance left behind is a larger share of what remains. Set your fragrance against wet batter weight and it quietly concentrates as the bar dries.

    So this page shows you both. The Ceiling control under Fragrance & additives on the Recipe tab lets you work in either basis, and the line beside it always reports your fragrance as a percentage of fat weight and as a percentage of the estimated cured bar. Compare that second figure against the Category 9 number on your certificate. Working in fat weight is the more cautious of the two for a normal recipe, which is why it's the default.
